A BEGINNER'S GUIDE TO MOUNTAINEERING

Everything we learned about climbing, famous peaks and staying safe, in one place.

Welcome to Alpine Ascents

We started this website because we wanted to understand mountaineering properly. For some people it means a long walk up to a viewpoint. For others it means weeks on snow and ice, trying to reach the top of a famous peak. Either way, it takes you to places very few people ever get to see.

While reading about it, one thing kept coming up: climbing is hard. You need strength, patience and a lot of preparation to deal with steep slopes, thin air and sudden changes in the weather. But almost every climber says the view from the top makes it all worth it.

The part we found most important is safety. Good planning, the right gear, proper training and respect for the mountain are what bring climbers home again. So on this site we have put together the basics, a few famous mountains and the skills every beginner should know.

Basics

History of Mountaineering

From the first ascent of Mont Blanc in 1786 to Everest in 1953, see how climbing grew into a worldwide sport.

Modern mountaineering began in 1786, when Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard reached the top of Mont Blanc in the Alps. The 1800s became the "Golden Age of Alpinism", when most big Alpine peaks were climbed for the first time. In the 1900s, climbers turned to the giants of the Himalaya and Karakoram.

  • 1786: first ascent of Mont Blanc, the start of modern mountaineering.
  • 1865: Edward Whymper's team reaches the top of the Matterhorn.
  • 1950: Annapurna becomes the first 8,000 m peak to be climbed.
  • 1953: Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay stand on top of Mount Everest.
  • 1954: an Italian expedition makes the first ascent of K2.

Did you know? There are 14 mountains higher than 8,000 m, and all of them are in Asia.

Basics

Types and Styles

Trekking, rock climbing, ice climbing and alpine style - each one needs different skills and gear.

Mountaineering covers many activities, from gentle hill walking to technical climbs on the highest peaks in the world. Most climbers start with trekking and slowly learn the harder styles.

  • Trekking: walking long trails through mountain areas, with no special climbing gear.
  • Rock climbing: climbing steep rock with hands, feet and a rope for safety.
  • Ice climbing: climbing frozen waterfalls and ice using ice axes and crampons.
  • Alpine style: a small team carries everything and climbs fast, without fixed camps.
  • Expedition style: a big team with fixed ropes and several camps, common on 8,000 m peaks.

Tip: beginners should start with guided treks before trying technical climbs.

Skills

Climbing Techniques

Basic rope work, belaying, using crampons and ice axes, and moving safely on steep ground.

Good technique saves energy and keeps climbers safe. These skills are learned step by step, best with an experienced teacher or a climbing club.

  • Belaying: controlling the rope for a partner so that a fall is stopped quickly.
  • Knots: the figure-eight knot is used to tie the rope to the harness.
  • Crampon technique: flat feet on gentle snow, front points on steep ice.
  • Self-arrest: using the ice axe to stop yourself sliding down a snow slope.
  • Rope teams: climbing roped together on glaciers in case someone falls into a crevasse.

Tip: practise self-arrest on a safe, gentle slope before you ever need it.

Skills

Navigation and Weather

Reading a map, using a compass and understanding mountain weather before it turns dangerous.

In the mountains, knowing where you are and what the weather will do is just as important as being fit. Weather can change in minutes, and clouds can hide the path completely.

  • Map and compass: every climber should be able to find the way without a phone.
  • GPS and offline maps: useful backups - keep the batteries warm in cold weather.
  • Weather forecasts: check them before the trip and again during the climb.
  • Turnaround time: choose a time to turn back, even if the summit looks close.
  • Whiteout: in thick cloud or snow, stop, stay together and move carefully.

Did you know? Temperature usually drops by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m you climb.

Gear

Gear and Equipment

Boots, layers, helmet, harness, rope, ice axe and crampons - the kit that keeps climbers safe.

The right gear makes climbing safer and more comfortable. Good equipment does not need to be the most expensive - it needs to fit well and suit the climb.

  • Boots: stiff, waterproof boots that crampons can be fixed to.
  • Layers: a base layer, a warm fleece or down jacket, and a waterproof shell.
  • Technical gear: helmet, harness, rope, carabiners, ice axe and crampons.
  • Light and navigation: map, compass and a headlamp with spare batteries.
  • Essentials: first-aid kit, sunglasses, sunscreen, water and high-energy food.

Tip: test all new gear on short, easy walks before a big climb.

Gear

Sheltering and Camping

How climbers set up base camps, choose safe tent spots and stay warm in cold conditions.

On long climbs, the camp is where climbers rest, eat and recover. A good camp can make the difference between a successful climb and turning back early.

  • Base camp: the main camp at the foot of the mountain, with most of the supplies.
  • High camps: smaller camps higher up, used for the final push to the summit.
  • Choosing a spot: flat ground, away from avalanche paths and falling rocks.
  • Staying warm: a four-season tent, an insulated sleeping mat and a warm sleeping bag.
  • Water: melt snow on a stove and drink plenty, even when you are not thirsty.

Tip: keep tomorrow's water bottle inside your sleeping bag so it does not freeze.

Safety

Mountain Hazards and Safety

Avalanches, storms, rockfall and crevasses - and the simple steps to avoid them.

Mountains are beautiful but they can be dangerous. Knowing the main hazards is the first step to avoiding them.

  • Avalanches: stay off steep snow slopes, especially after heavy snowfall.
  • Crevasses: hidden cracks in glaciers - always travel roped together.
  • Rockfall: wear a helmet and move quickly through risky areas.
  • Storms and cold: check the forecast and watch for frostbite and hypothermia.
  • Tell someone: always share your route and the time you plan to be back.

Golden rule: the summit is optional - getting home safely is not.

Safety

Altitude and Acclimatization

Why the air gets thin up high, what altitude sickness is, and how climbers adjust slowly.

The higher you go, the less oxygen there is in each breath. The body can adapt, but only if it is given enough time. This slow adjustment is called acclimatization.

  • Altitude sickness: headache, nausea and tiredness, often starting above about 2,500 m.
  • Climb high, sleep low: go higher during the day and come down to sleep.
  • Go slowly: above 3,000 m, a common rule is to sleep only 300-500 m higher each night.
  • Drink and rest: plenty of water, good food and rest days help the body adjust.
  • Danger signs: confusion or breathlessness while resting mean go down right away.

Did you know? At the top of Everest, each breath has only about one third of the oxygen found at sea level.
